House Marrow

Holds the largest port on the Drowned Coast, the chain across its headlands, and the weighing house every grain must pass through. Sets the toll. Enforces it.

Great house Seat: Marrow Bay 12 pages

House Marrow was a shipping family before the fall and is a customs authority now, though it would object to the description. Its claim on the bay predates the essence trade by two centuries; its claim on the essence trade rests entirely on owning the only deepwater channel where essence comes ashore in quantity.

The house is run by a council of seven, five of whom are Marrows by blood. It is competent, unsentimental and unusually consistent — which is why the toll arrangement has survived thirty-one years and three attempts to break it.

How it makes money

Three ways, in order of size: the toll on displacement, the weighing house's cut of every grain assayed, and the licences it sells to the mudlark villages on the flats. It does not refine and it does not ship inland, which is the arrangement it has with House Calder and the source of the friction between them.

Marrow's weakness is that it owns a chokepoint rather than a product. Every year Calder investigates whether a second channel could be dredged somewhere else on the coast, and every year the survey comes back saying no. One of these years it will come back saying yes.

Dealing with the house

Marrow keeps its word and expects the same, in writing, with a witness. It does not take offence and it does not forgive debt. A party that pays the toll without complaint will find the house genuinely helpful; a party that evades it once will find every subsequent conversation on the coast slightly more expensive.

Calder's survey came back positive eighteen months ago. Two members of the Marrow council know, because they were paid to know, and they have been quietly moving the house's holdings out of the bay ever since.

The Synod also knows what fell at Marrow Bay last season, and it does not match the assay records the weighing house filed. It is waiting to see who talks first. If the party surfaces the toll ledger — three houses named on one page that should never have appeared together — both of these secrets become public in the same week.
