The premise

A god died in the sky, and the pieces are still falling

One page, no wiki required. What happened, who is left, and why anyone would sail out into it.

A century ago something enormous came apart above the world. Nobody agrees on what it was. Everybody agrees on what it left: light that falls out of the sky in grains, burns cold, and can be spent.

The scholars at the Synod call it essence and write about it carefully. The houses call it revenue. Dockhands at Marrow Bay call it fallweight, weigh it on tea scales, and sell it to whoever is standing there when the tide goes out.

What actually changed

Divinity became a commodity. Prayer still works, more or less, but so does a vial of ground essence and a steady hand. That single fact bent every institution in the world out of shape within two generations. Temples that once brokered miracles now compete with a refinery. Twelve merchant houses that used to move grain now move light under armed escort. Three cartels grew up around the shipping lanes because the sea is where most of it lands, and the sea does not care about charters.

The world is not post-apocalyptic. It is worse than that in some ways and much better in others: it is busy. Ports are being built. Claims are being filed. Somebody is always financing an expedition to a place that appeared on no chart last season.

The short version

A dead god's remains are the world's most valuable resource. Everyone is in the business of collecting them. Your table decides whether that is a salvage job, a trade war, a pilgrimage, or a heist.

Who is left

Seven gods still answer, and their answers have grown strange. Some are diminished and know it. Some are pretending nothing happened, which is its own kind of tell. One has stopped speaking to clergy entirely and communicates only through the tide. What they all share is a problem: their authority now has a market price, and markets are not reverent.

The houses

House Marrow holds the bay that carries its name and the toll that comes with it. House Calder holds the refineries inland and would very much like the bay. Ten more houses sit between them in various states of alliance and grievance. None of them is the villain. All of them will use you.

The Synod

A scholarly body with a licensing arm, which is the polite way of saying it decides who may legally study essence. It is not evil. It is understaffed, badly frightened, and holding a monopoly it never asked for.

The tide came in with a reliquary and went out with two of my crew. The ledger only has a column for one of those.

— a toll captain's deposition, Marrow Bay

What it plays like

Starfall is 5e compatible and written for tables that like consequences. Essence is the pressure: it funds you, it changes you if you handle it badly, and someone always wants the vial you are carrying. Sessions tend to look like salvage runs, negotiations that turn, and journeys across water that behaves like it remembers something.

Nothing here requires a rules rewrite. You need the core rules you already own, a coastline, and a reason to be on it.

Where most of it lands

The sea got the largest share

Most of what fell fell in water, and the water kept the shape of it. Currents run against the wind. Charts expire. The cartel prices passage by risk rather than distance, and the risk is honest.

Common questions

Before you commit a table to it

Do I need to read the wiki?
No. This page is the whole premise. The wiki is there for the session where somebody asks what house runs the refinery.
Is it 5e compatible?
Yes. Written and tested for fifth edition at open tables. No subsystem replaces a core rule; essence sits on top of what you already run.
What level does it start at?
Level one works. The published adventures cover levels 1–10, and the quickstart begins at level one on the Drowned Coast.
How grim is it?
Grim in the way a working port is grim. People are poor, houses are ruthless, and the sea is genuinely dangerous — but nothing here is bleak for its own sake.
Can I use it with my own setting?
Yes, and people do. The essence economy is portable: drop the falling light on any coastline and the houses will invent themselves.
